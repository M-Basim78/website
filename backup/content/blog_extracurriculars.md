### How to Navigate Extracurricular Activities as a Pre-Med or Medical Student

(Video coming soon)

Extracurricular activities are an expected part of applying to medical school and residency—but figuring out which ones actually matter (and how many is “enough”) can feel overwhelming. Many students fall into the trap of overloading their schedules with activities that look good on paper, only to end up burned out and stretched too thin.

In this post, I’ll break down the different types of extracurriculars, how to choose opportunities that genuinely strengthen your application, and how to be intentional so your involvement tells a clear story—without sacrificing your academics or well-being. Whether you’re a pre-med just getting started or a medical student refining your CV, this guide will help you focus on what truly adds value.

Choosing the Right Activities: Quality Over Quantity

When it comes to extracurriculars, there’s no single “perfect” checklist—but there is a common theme among strong applications: intentional involvement. Activities should support your growth, align with your values, and tell a clear story about who you are and why medicine makes sense for you.

Core Categories of Activities

Most extracurriculars fall into a few broad buckets:

- Leadership

- Volunteer work

- Employment

- Research

- Shadowing

Rather than trying to do everything, focus on doing a consistently.

few things well and

Guiding Principles for Choosing Activities

- Do what you love. If you’re not passionate about it, it will show—and burnout will follow.

- Choose activities that are in service of others, especially communities in need.

- Seek opportunities to work with diverse patient populations.

- Work in team-based and interprofessional environments.

- Demonstrate different forms of leadership (formal and informal).

- Only continue activities that bring you purpose, joy, and meaning.

- Practice balancing academics with extracurriculars—grades still matter.

- Learn when to say “no” and when it’s okay to step back.

- Show long-term commitment by staying involved over multiple years whenever possible.

### Paid Work/Job

Because I needed to work to afford college, I was intentional about choosing jobs that aligned with medicine. If you have to work (and many students do), your job can be a strength rather than a limitation.

Medical, health care, or patient-facing roles provide valuable insight into healthcare systems, teamwork, and patient care—while also demonstrating responsibility and resilience.

### Research

Turn What You’re Already Doing Into Scholarship

- Research doesn’t always have to start in a lab. One of my favorite strategies is turning existing activities into academic projects.

- Examples include:

- Conducting surveys (e.g., of volunteers or participants) and creating posters

- Community survey collection

Health needs assessments

Writing reports or reflections on volunteer initiatives

Quality Improvement Projects in Clinic & Hospital

- Developing pre- and post-surveys for presentations or educational sessions

- Writing case reports & posters on interesting patients (with appropriate supervision and consent)

- Submitting Op-Eds or Op-Meds reflecting on personal experiences in healthcare

- Presenting posters or talks at local, regional, or national conferences

- This approach shows curiosity, initiative, and the ability to reflect critically on your experiences.

### Shadowing:

For shadowing, I leaned into connections I made through work and volunteering. Shadowing different providers allowed me to explore specialties, understand team dynamics, and confirm my interest in medicine—without treating shadowing as a box-checking exercise.

### Mentors

- Reach out to key mentors frequently

- Especially when in crises (LOA, health problems, failures, etc)

- During important milestones (STEP score, Honors, Conference, Publications)

- Meet them in person to go over strategies

- Keep them up to date on accomplishments, struggles, and challenges

- Assist with Research Ideas especially interesting patients

### Conferences:

- Present research posters, talks, and submit abstracts

- Attend all Residency Fairs and give the residents your contact information

- Network and form connections - easier in person

- Do a mix of virtual & In-person

### Tying Your Extracurriculars Into Your Application

Pro Tip

Choose you genuinely care about and commit long-term. Depth, reflection, and service to others matter far more than checking every box. Then over time take on a leadership role. Lastly, turn the project into research & present at a conference!

one or two volunteer roles

Waiving Extracurriculars into Your Story

Your extracurriculars are not meant to stand alone—they exist to support your . Medical schools are not looking for a list of impressive titles; they are looking for evidence that your experiences have shaped how you think, how you serve others, and why medicine is the right path for you.

story

When writing your focus less on what you did and more on what you learned. The strongest narratives show growth: how working with vulnerable populations deepened your empathy, how team-based roles taught you communication and humility, or how leadership experiences helped you navigate responsibility and accountability. Your activities should naturally reinforce the themes already present in your story.

personal statement,

Ask yourself:

- Which experiences best explain why I want to be a physician?

- How did these activities challenge or change me?

- What moments confirmed that medicine aligns with my values?

- Your personal statement should feel like the throughline connecting your extracurriculars—demonstrating consistency, reflection, and purpose. When your activities, reflections, and motivations align, admissions committees can clearly see not only that you are prepared for medicine, but why you belong in it.

: a strong application isn’t built on doing more—it’s built on doing what matters, staying committed, and being able to thoughtfully articulate how those experiences shaped you.
