### My Step1 Dedicated Experience:

From the first month of my M2 year I tried to do some UW to review. However, this led me to get behind on my curriculum so I changed to only doing questions correlating with the curriculum we were currently learning.

At my Medical College in the Spring of our M2 year we are given the First Aid Step 1 book and provided 2 months (8 weeks) of "Dedicated" study time where we were supposed to study and take STEP1.

At my school there is specialized tutoring services and extra NBME practice exam vouchers BUT YOU NEED TO ASK for them.

February was our "complex cases" block. I thought I should start doing more UW review so I did more and did not spend enough time on learning the new materials in this unit. This led me to fail the summative and then spend 2 weeks of my dedicated STEP1 studying period retaking it. Thankfully I passed the summative exam the second time around. However, this led me to only have 6 weeks to take Step1 before my M3 year started.

Dedicated Time:

From my first couple practice exams during my “dedicated time” I realized that I did not have a strong foundation of the material. Therefore, I used the First Aid Chapters and Sketchy Videos to determine write down a list which topics I was least comfortable to most comfortable with. I then focussed on the topics that I needed more time on.

### First Study Strategy: Re-learn Material

Strategies to re-learn and learn the material:

- Each day I would focus on reading, watching review videos and doing some questions on a set topic.

- Best and number one reading source that everyone recommended was First Aid. Which I usually could read and digest about 10 pages per hour.

- Other readings I acquired and kept on hand: Crush Step1, Dirty USMLE Rocks, Rapid Review, Golden High Yield

- For for review I used Osmosis, Boards&Beyond, Sketchy, and Pixorize depending on the topic.

videos

[OnlineMedEd (25% off with code medpsycmoss25),](https://www.onlinemeded.com/pricing?utm_campaign=unified-no-account&utm_medium=web&utm_source=unified-login&_gl=1*jfdphx*_gcl_au*MTg5ODc2MTY5OS4xNzIxODMzNzgz*_ga*NzYzMzg4MDMwLjE3MjE4MzM3ODI.*_ga_DQJHY299L7*MTcyMTgzMzc4Mi4xLjEuMTcyMTgzMzc4NC41OC4wLjA.)

- I tired Osmosis, Boards & Beyond, and Amboss questions but the so focused on them and did others if wanted more practice questions.

questions most similar to the actual exam was UWorld

- I did UWorld on one topic in tutur mode at a time. At first I could only do 40 Q in tutor mode (4 hours) per day to allow enough time to read and watch videos in the afternoon (4-6 hours). Over time I was able to do 20Q in tutor mode in an hour because I was getting more right and could go onto the next step.

- When I received around 50-60% correct I knew that I knew the material and could move on.

Downsides to this strategy:

- Through only focussing on one topic at a time I would not get the practice of seeing questions mixed just like the exam.

- This strategy took a LONG time because each major topic has many questions which can take almost a week to do if only doing 40 Questions a day.

- I also struggled with anxiety that I had to finish all the questions on each topic which lead to me a longer time to get through them before I would move on to another material I was uncomfortable with.

- Reading, podcasts, and watching videos is not active enough and is not what the exam is like so to some extent it can waste precious question time. The hour of video/reading could have been 10 UWorld questions learning it as in depth as you need.

- I would try to take one practice exam every week to see my progress. Unfortunately my scores barely improved each week because I either didn’t know all the material yet and/or I hadn’t practiced in mixed timed mode.

Transition to mixed questions:

- First, 40Q mixed in tutor mode.

- Originally I would do 40 questions in 4 hours (1hr per 10 Q), but over time I got it down to review 40 Q in 2 hours.

- Second, 40Q mixed in timed mode (1 hour). Then review the questions which took 3-4hours.

### What happens if you fail?

At most US Medical schools you must TAKE and PASS this exam between the 2nd and 3rd year of Medical School (some schools after your 3rd year). However, every school is a bit different on their requirements, resources, and expectations.

At my US MD medical school there is an opportunity to ask for an extension of time to study for STEP. One option is using the first month of M3 Clerkships (May) as your 4 week elective. However, if you don't take it during this time, you fail, or need more time you will have to take a Leave of Absence (LOA). At my school at least 5 people did this in my class alone.

If you you have the opportunity to study and retake Step 1 again for a second time. However, each school is different on how many times they will allow you to fail before sending you in front of the Medical School Board to offer you the opportunity to take it a 3rd or 4th time before dismissal from medical school. The AAMC only allows you to retake the Step1 exam 6 times.

Fail Step 1

I hate that word, "failing." That just means you didn't score the minimum that the school or Academic Board put in place. The NBME decided that a "Pass" was a score of 196 on the STEP 1 exam and a 214 on the STEP2 exam .

If you score below that or "Fail," first YOU WILL BE OK! From my experience talking to other medical students and reading the AAMC report, at least 6% of medical students fail on their first attempt!

From the AAMC: "No, a Step 1 failure won’t doom your medical career"

- "In 2017, 72% of U.S. MD applicants (including both U.S. MD seniors and prior graduates) who failed Step 1 on the first attempt went on to a residency, compared to 94% of applicants who passed on the first attempt."

- "However, we know from longitudinal data that within six years after graduation from medical school, more than 99% of all graduates have records of graduate medical education training and/or are in practice in the United States."

- This means that at least 6% of medical students fail Step 1, and if you keep trying and graduate you will get into residency

### Second Study Strategy: Practice Questions

During the Second round at studying for Step1, I knew it was time try a new strategy for studying.

- Every day I did two 40Q mixed (80 Q in 2 hours) back to back with a 5-10min break in between then review (6-8hours)

- Then three 40Q mixed (120Q in 2 hours) back to back with 5-10min break in between then review (9-12hours). Obviously this started to be impossible to do 120Q every day plus reviewing them so I only did this a couple times a week. However, when I did this my scores consistently improved! The days I felt most successful was when I did 80 Q in the morning back to back then reviewed them because I was not overworking myself but still got a significant amount of practice.

- I did all the practice tests available and eventually had to do them all twice since I ran out of practice tests.

### The Social Determinants of Learning:

This is a great time to briefly touch on how the Social Determinants of Learning (SDOL) affect student academic success. The Social Determinants of Learning include 6 main categories: physical health, psychosocial health, physical environment, social environment, economic stability and self-motivation. When students suffer from these social and health inequities they are more likely to struggle with higher educational requirements including doing well on standardized exams (like STEP).

Read more includding the published papers by the Chamberlin University

[https://www.chamberlain.edu/about/social-determinants-of-learning](https://www.chamberlain.edu/about/social-determinants-of-learning)

[https://www.chamberlain.edu/social-determinants-of-learning/Primer](https://www.chamberlain.edu/social-determinants-of-learning/Primer)

SODL Challenges during my STEP1 Studying:

We are all human and still have to live life while we go through medical school. Following, the world doesn't stop when are studying for the board exams. It is important to recognize when you need to slow down and take a moment to address those external stressors before going back to your educational stressors. Sometimes this means taking a day off from studying, but sometimes one must recognize when it is time to take more time off. For me it was best to take a leave of absence (LOA) between my second and third year of medical school. However, what I didn't realize was not only was I adding an additional year of my medical training, but had to deal with the financial and health consequences of taking an LOA:

Financial —> not being able to pay for food, healthcare or rent Worked 20-40 hours while also studying Stress Exasperated health challenges Emotional and relationship challenges of watching my husband & friends go through their M3 year without me Unsupportive mentors: “you should have been studying 100% of the time not working”
