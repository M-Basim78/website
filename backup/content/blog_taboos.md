### 1. Mental health

I’m going to start with mental health because if you are human, you have a mind, and will struggle at some point in dealing with your mental health. In medicine, we frequently over emphasize the body’s physical health because we can physically conceptualize it through physiology, anatomy, chemistry, pathology, etc. However, we cannot put thoughts and emotions on a slide and look under it on a microscope.

Rarely do academic medical school curriculums emphasize mental health as a pertinent portion of an individuals’ health. Though I am not here to talk that, I’m here to talk about about our mental health as future health care providers.

As medical students we are immediately thrown into the deep end of the pool where we must learn to swim, if not suffer the consequences. In a short period of time we are given countless medical diagnosis, physiological and biochemical pathways, medications and their mechanisms of action that we must learn how to quickly “drink from a fire hose.” We are then frequently tested, graded, and judged on our intellect based on how quickly we are able to spit out and regurgitate information we most recently learned.

As you see below through the rest of the taboos, there are multiple types of struggles medical students experience that inertly impact their mental health. I want to emphasize in this number one taboo that YOU ARE NOT ALONE! Everyone is struggling with their mental health whether or not they are in a place where they are able to acknowledge or recognize it.

This is all a part of being human.

There will be moments when we notice our mental struggles. Sometimes they come out as emotions which can become overwhelming, especially if we have been pushing them down for so long in order to focus on our academics or patient care.

These emotions can become so severe and poor out as anger, sadness, frustration, or even as apathy. In this last emotion one might consider giving up on medicine or even our life. I want to say that it is normal to have these thoughts and emotions, even though the might scare you. You are not a bad person for thinking these things. You are just under a lot of pressure and your body is reacting in response.

However, if these emotions are not addressed then they can become overwhelming and one might start acting out on these emotions.

Once you feel yourself coming up with a specific plan or these emotions become so severe that it is all you can think about PLEASE TELL SOMEONE. This can be a friend, peer, teacher, parent,

988 or 911

This is the time where you must be brave and ask for help one more time. I know it is very hard to ask for help. Trust me, I know, especially coming from a traumatic background, where I didn’t know I had anyone to ask for help.

Know that right now you do have someone, whether if that’s me or someone else.

You are worthy.

You have a purpose in this world.

There is at least one person who cares and believes in you.

You are not alone!

### 2. Challenges with Academics & Imposter Syndrome:

According to the , in 2021-2022 cycle 62,443 individuals applied to medical school for just around 22,000 positions in the US. This means that 35% were accepted.

[AAMC](https://www.aamc.org/media/57761/download?attachment)

In 2021, 11,576 pre-medical students applied to in Chicago. Out of those interviewed and selected only around 152 began Rush as an M1 (first year) this year. That means only 1.3% were selected. If you are one of these 1%, CONGRATULATIONS!

[Rush Medical College](https://www.rushu.rush.edu/rush-medical-college/about-rush-medical-college)

Keep this 1% number close to your heart because there will be times (more than you can count) where you will feel stupid, unworthy, dazed, etc. This will happen because you are now in a group of individuals who are part of the 1%. DO NOT get worked up when you do not get the "top" grade. It will always be a bell curve, be happy if you get an "average" or "mean" score because that is it, it is the average of the 1% of your class. If your school is in the Pass/Fail System (Thank you Rush!!!) then ALL YOU NEED IS TO PASS! That means that at the end of the academic block all your grades will be added together and you have to get above the designated "pass" number, usually above 70%.

If you are struggling to understand something, so is at least 50% of your classmates! Reach out and ask for help from your own classmates, upper classmates, and professors. Each school usually has a service so ask your academic advising team early! Don't wait till you fail an exam. go to

### 4.

[Accommodations- physical & mental health disabilities](https://medpsycmoss.com/blog/disability-accommodations-and-accessibility)

All medical schools and exams are required to have some sort of accommodations and accessibility available. However, not all schools value this or spread the word that they exist. I unfortunately, found out I could apply to receive accommodations after over 2 years of medical school, failing 2 courses, and failing my STEP1 Exam.

If you struggle with any of the following things (non-inclusive), consider talking to your

[School's Accommodations Representative:](https://www.rush.edu/disability-rights-and-accommodations)

Not finishing quizzes or exams in given time (have to skip over questions and would benefit for an additional 5+ more minutes)

Needing to take a bathroom, water, medication, snack break during an exam and not given break time

Suffering from Testing Anxiety

Have a diagnosed physical, medical, or mental health disability

Have a diagnosed learning disability

Have a mental health condition that impacts your focus while in class or taking an exam (ADHD, Anxiety, Depression, PTSD, etc)

Have medical or mental health condition where you need time off for therapy or medical appointments

If you are breast feeding

If you need time in private to do medical care (ex: injections)

If you need a quieter room with less people during exams

If you need to sit in a chair during rounds or can't stand for longer than 30+ minutes at a time.

If you have an acute injury or illness that will impact your academics longer than 1 week

Need time off during the day for medical appointments, medications, self care during clinicals

### 5.

[Taking a Leave of Absence (LOA):](https://medpsycmoss.com/blog/leave-of-absence)

There are two types of Leave of Absence in Medical School: or When one takes a LOA from medical school, one must sign a piece of paper from administration check marking one of these two options:

Academic

Personal

An is when the school decides you must take a leave because you have failed to many courses, have to retake a year course, or have to retake a board exam. This is seen not as favorably by residencies (so I have been told).

Academic LOA

A Personal LOA can be for any reason and it is because you choose to take the leave. It can be because of family obligations, health concerns, research, or just needing a break. You do not need to explain why to the school, but will need to explain what you spent your time doing during this time in your Residency Application and Interview.

Things to consider when taking an LOA:

- How will you finance the months off? You are not allowed to take out Federal Student loans when you are on LOA

- How will you finance Health Insurance? Some schools allow you to remain on the same plan but you will need to pay out of pocket. Our insurance cost us around $4,000 for 9 months off of school.

- Does your school provide you continued access to academic study spaces and tutoring services? My school does when individuals are on LOA.

- Where will you live?

- Will you need to work?

- Do you qualify for any financial aid assistance through city, state, or federal programs?

- Are there any current or previous students at your school who took an LOA and can provide advice? We have created a GroupMe for Medical Students in our school with around 20 students who have been on or are currently on an LOA- reach out to Stephanie
