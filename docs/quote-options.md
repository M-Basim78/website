# Quotes for the new site — your words, and some alternatives to consider

**For: Stephanie Moss, MD**
**From: the web build**

## What this is

While going through the photo folder you shared, we found four "Business Story of
the Week" cards carrying quotes attributed to you. They are genuinely yours, which
solved a problem: the current homepage carries a line about humanizing medicine
that your existing site attributes to **Gabor Maté, MD (*The Myth of Normal*)**, not
to you. Using your own words there is both more accurate and more powerful.

**The site currently ships your words exactly as written.** Nothing below has been
published. These are drafts for you to reject, pick from, or ignore. You are the
writer here; we are only offering a second pass on rhythm and specificity.

One house rule the new site follows, which shapes the suggestions: **no em dashes
or en dashes in visible copy.** Sentences are split, or use a comma, colon or full
stop instead.

---

## 1. "Humanizing medicine means seeing the person, not just the patient."

**This is the one we recommend for the homepage**, in the Patient and Doctor
section, replacing the Maté line. It is thematically exact for that part of the
page and it is the strongest of the four as written.

### What it already does well
A clean antithesis, and "not just" is doing real clinical work: it says *also*
see the patient, not *instead of*. That nuance is worth protecting.

### Alternatives

| | Version | Why you might prefer it |
|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Humanizing medicine means seeing the person **before** the patient. | Sharper, and closer to trauma-informed practice: the order matters. **Caution:** it changes the meaning. It now implies sequence rather than addition, which some clinicians will read as downplaying the clinical work. Only use if you mean it. |
| **A2** | Humanizing medicine means seeing the person the patient still is. | Keeps "also, not instead", removes the slightly soft "not just". Reads more like a line of prose than a slogan. |
| **A3** | We humanize medicine by humanizing the people inside it, patients and providers both. | Widest scope. Echoes the sentiment your site already carries and covers clinicians as well as patients, which is most of your actual audience. Longer, so better as a paragraph opener than a display quote. |

**Our pick: keep yours as written.** It is already the best of the four and the
alternatives trade nuance for punch.

## 2. "My path was all about trial, error, failure, and keep going."

### What it already does well
The grammar deliberately breaks. Three nouns then a verb phrase. That wobble is
the most *you* thing in the set, and it lands because it stops sounding composed
at exactly the moment it describes not being composed.

### Alternatives

| | Version | Why you might prefer it |
|---|---|---|
| **B1** | Trial. Error. Failure. Keep going. | Strongest as a display line. Turns the broken parallel into a deliberate rhythm rather than a slip. Excellent over a hero image; too clipped for body copy. |
| **B2** | My path was trial, error, failure, and going anyway. | Fixes the grammar while keeping the shape. Safer, slightly less alive. |
| **B3** | My path was trial, error, failure, and refusing to stop. | Most conventional. Included for completeness; we think it is the weakest, because it explains the feeling instead of enacting it. |

**Our pick: B1** for a display slot, yours as written for anywhere it sits in a
paragraph.

## 3. "It's not about what life throws at you, but how you rise above it."

### Honest note
This is the one we would most encourage you to revisit. It is close to a very
familiar saying, and next to the other three it is the least recognisably yours.
Everything else in the set could only have been written by someone who lived it.
This one could have been written by anyone.

The fix is not better phrasing, it is **specificity**. Your actual story is far
more compelling than the general principle it is standing in for.

### Alternatives

| | Version | Why you might prefer it |
|---|---|---|
| **C1** | The setback is not the story. What you build afterwards is. | Keeps your meaning, drops the cliché, and stays short enough for a card. |
| **C2** | Medicine will measure you by your worst week. You are more than that week. | Specific to your field and your experience. The strongest of the three, and the most quotable. |
| **C3** | Failing an exam is an event. It is not an identity. | Closest to what you actually teach, and it names the thing directly. Reads as advice rather than aphorism, which may suit a blog header better than a hero quote. |

**Our pick: C2.**

---

## 4. "We need to see the humanity in others, even when it's hard to see it in ourselves."

### What it already does well
It has a genuine turn in it. The second half reframes the first, and it lands on
self-compassion without ever using that word. This is the most sophisticated of
the four.

### Alternatives

| | Version | Why you might prefer it |
|---|---|---|
| **D1** | See the humanity in others, **especially** when it is hard to see in yourself. | "Especially" is stronger than "even when": it makes the difficulty the reason rather than the exception. |
| **D2** | We see humanity in others long before we manage it for ourselves. | Turns it from instruction into observation. Gentler, and truer to how it actually goes. |

**Caution on D1:** it swaps "we/ourselves" for "you/yourself". Your original speaks
as a physician standing *with* colleagues; the alternative speaks *to* them. That
is a real change in register, and the inclusive version may be the better fit for
your audience. Worth a moment's thought rather than a quick call.

**Our pick: yours as written**, or D1 if you want more force.
