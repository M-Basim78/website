Implicit biases are deeply rooted, unconscious attitudes or stereotypes that can influence our judgments, actions, and decisions. When I was tasked with taking an implicit bias test focused on transgender individuals for the Gender Affirmation Elective, I found myself feeling quite nervous. Despite my conscious efforts to treat everyone equally and with respect, a tiny voice inside me questioned whether my subconscious biases might differ from my conscious beliefs.

Test your own Implicit bias on various topics by

The Project Implicit here: https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/selectatest.html

I distinctly recall my introduction to individuals with diverse gender expressions, sexual identities, and variations in sexual organs during my 10th-grade psychology class. A group of students representing these identities, most of whom were older than me presented to my class. Among them, one individual stood out - a person who identified as intersex. They presented themselves as outwardly feminine, but when they disclosed their intersex status, I was taken aback. It was a revelation to me that someone could be born with multiple sexual organs, allowing them to express the gender they felt most comfortable with in their own body.

Another eye-opening experience occurred when I spoke with the parent of one of my martial arts students, a five-year-old boy who had chosen to wear nail polish at the studio. I was initially concerned that other students might ridicule them. To my surprise, the parent responded by emphasizing that they couldn't control what others thought. Their primary concern was their child's happiness and the freedom to make choices that were not harmful. This encounter challenged my preconceived notions about children's agency in making decisions about their appearance, which contrasted with my own upbringing, where my parents had imposed strict rules about how I dressed. To my parent they always wanted to me to dress “feminine” which included make-up, hair, and dresses. Even though I secretly liked dresses, I was bothered that I had to wear them. None of the other children had to wear them, and they prevented me from adequately climbing the playground structure.

I vividly remember a subsequent encounter during my pre-medical training, when I shadowed a doctor who allowed their own pre-teen child to begin hormone therapy to prevent the development of breasts. This was an entirely new concept to me, and I questioned its safety. The doctor assured me that extensive research supported the therapy's safety and that the child could discontinue it if they chose to develop breasts later. More than a decade later, this individual has blossomed into their true beautiful self, a testament to how such decisions can positively impact a person's life.

These are just a few of the many experiences that have shaped my perspective before I entered medical school. These interactions with the transgender community have left an enduring mark on my heart. I believe that these encounters are a significant reason why my implicit bias tests have indicated an equal bias towards both cisgender and transgender individuals.
